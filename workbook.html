Workbook E0–E9

Breadboard workbook

Ten experiments, E0 to E9. Each one teaches one idea from the primer. Each experiment has the same steps: goal → parts → predict → build → measure → explain → Arduino link.

Do the "predict" step before you build anything. The point is to compare what you calculated with what the meter shows.

Safety

  • Use only batteries. Never connect anything in this workbook to mains power.
  • Never connect a wire directly between + and −. This is a short circuit. The wire and the batteries become hot.
  • Never put the meter in current mode across the battery. In current mode, the meter is almost a wire. It makes a short circuit, and its fuse blows.
  • Turn the battery pack off before you change the circuit.
  • Check LED and capacitor polarity. A capacitor that is connected the wrong way can become hot and break open.
  • If a part becomes hot, turn off the battery pack immediately.

How to use your multimeter

Most meters have three sockets: - COM: the black lead always goes here. - VΩ (often VΩmA): the red lead goes here for voltage, resistance and continuity. - mA (sometimes combined with VΩ) and 10A: the red lead goes here for current. In this workbook, always use the mA socket.

Voltage (V⎓, DC): measure across a part. 1. Set the dial to DC volts, 20 V range (or auto). 2. Put the red probe on the side of the part that is nearer to +. 3. Put the black probe on the other side. 4. A negative number means that the probes are the wrong way round. The value is still correct.

Current (A⎓, mA): measure in the path. The current must go through the meter. 1. Turn off the battery pack. 2. Move the red lead to the mA socket. 3. Set the dial to DC mA, 200 mA range. 4. Open the circuit at one point. Usually, you remove one jumper wire. 5. Connect the red probe to the side nearer to +. Connect the black probe to the other side. 6. Turn on the battery pack and read the value. 7. Move the red lead back to the VΩ socket. If you forget, your next voltage measurement makes a short circuit.

Resistance (Ω): measure with the power off, and preferably with the part out of the circuit.

Continuity (the sound symbol): the meter beeps if two points are connected.

Tip: meter probes are often too thick for breadboard holes. Put a short jumper wire in the hole. Touch the probe to the free end of the jumper.

How to read the layouts

  • The rows are numbered 1 to 63. The columns are a–e (left half) and f–j (right half).
  • In each row, the five holes a–e are connected. The five holes f–j are connected. The centre gap separates the two halves.
  • The long rails on the sides connect along the full length. In this workbook, the left rails are the power rails: + (red line) = 6 V, − (blue line) = GND.
  • "a10" means column a, row 10.

Put the battery pack's red wire into the + rail and the black wire into the − rail. Keep the pack off until a step tells you to turn it on.

E0 — Meet the meter

Goal: measure a battery voltage and a resistor value.

Parts: battery pack, one resistor of each value (220 Ω, 330 Ω, 1 kΩ, 10 kΩ).

Predict: an AA cell is 1.5 V. What will four in series give? Read the colour bands on each resistor (colour code) and write the value that you expect.

Measure: 1. Set the meter to DC volts. 2. Measure one AA cell. Then measure the full pack between its red and black wires. 3. Set the meter to Ω. 4. Measure each resistor. Hold the legs with the probes, not with your fingers. Your body adds a parallel resistance.

Predicted Measured
One AA cell 1.5 V
Pack (4 × AA) 6.0 V
220 Ω 220 Ω
330 Ω 330 Ω
1 kΩ 1000 Ω
10 kΩ 10 000 Ω

Explain: new alkaline cells give 1.55–1.6 V, so the pack can give 6.2–6.4 V. Resistors have a tolerance. A gold band means ±5 %, and a brown fifth band means ±1 %. A 330 Ω (±5 %) resistor can measure from 313 Ω to 347 Ω.

Arduino link: an Arduino needs a stable 5 V. Its regulator makes 5 V from a higher, changing battery voltage.

E1 — Map the breadboard

Goal: find out which holes are connected inside the breadboard.

Parts: breadboard, two jumper wires. No battery.

Predict: which holes are connected to a10? Which are connected to f10?

Measure: 1. Set the meter to continuity. 2. Put one jumper in a10 and one in e10. Touch the probes to the free ends. Write "beep" or "no beep". 3. Repeat for: a10–a11, e10–f10, f10–j10. 4. Repeat for two holes at the top and the bottom of the same rail (rows 1 and 63).

Pair Predicted Measured
a10–e10 beep
a10–a11 no beep
e10–f10 no beep
f10–j10 beep
+ rail, top–bottom beep

Explain: each row half is one metal strip. The centre gap separates the halves, so a chip can sit across it. On some boards, the rails have a break in the middle. A gap in the red and blue lines shows the break. If you find one, connect the two halves with a jumper.

Arduino link: every circuit in the starter kit uses this layout.

E2 — Ohm's law

Goal: check that V = I × R.

Parts: battery pack, 330 Ω, 1 kΩ, 10 kΩ, jumper wires.

Predict (with 6.0 V):

Resistor I = V / R
330 Ω 18.2 mA
1 kΩ 6.0 mA
10 kΩ 0.6 mA

Build: 1. Put the 330 Ω resistor between the + rail and a10. 2. Put a jumper from c10 to the − rail.

Measure: 1. Turn on the pack. Measure the voltage between the + rail and the − rail. 2. Turn off the pack. Remove the jumper from c10. 3. Set up the meter for current (red lead in mA). 4. Put a jumper in c10. Touch the red probe to its free end. Touch the black probe to the − rail. 5. Turn on the pack. Read the current. 6. Turn off the pack. Repeat steps 2–5 with 1 kΩ and 10 kΩ. 7. Move the red lead back to VΩ.

Resistor V measured I measured V / I
330 Ω
1 kΩ
10 kΩ

Explain: V / I gives the resistance. Your values will be a few percent different from the labels, because of the tolerance, the real battery voltage, and the meter's own small resistance. The 330 Ω resistor uses 109 mW. It can feel slightly warm.

Arduino link: an Arduino pin at 5 V with a 330 Ω load supplies 15 mA. This is inside the safe limit of about 20 mA.

E3 — LED and resistor

Goal: see why an LED needs a resistor, and calculate it.

Parts: battery pack, red LED, 220 Ω, 330 Ω, 1 kΩ, jumpers.

Predict: I = (6 − 2.0) / R.

Resistor LED current
220 Ω 18.2 mA
330 Ω 12.1 mA
1 kΩ 4.0 mA

Build:

  1. Put the 330 Ω resistor between the + rail and a10.
  2. Put the LED long leg (anode) in c10 and the short leg (cathode) in c11.
  3. Put a jumper from a11 to the − rail.
  4. Turn on the pack. The LED is on.

Measure: 1. Measure the voltage across the LED (red probe row 10, black probe row 11). 2. Measure the voltage across the resistor (red probe + rail, black probe row 10). 3. Turn off the pack. Remove the jumper from a11. Measure the current with the meter between a11 and the − rail. 4. Repeat with 220 Ω and 1 kΩ. Compare the brightness. 5. Turn the LED around. Does it light?

Resistor V LED V resistor Current Brightness
220 Ω
330 Ω
1 kΩ

Explain: the LED voltage changes only a little (about 1.8–2.1 V), while the current changes by a factor of four. The resistor takes the remaining voltage. V LED + V resistor = battery voltage (Kirchhoff's voltage law). When the LED is reversed, no current flows. A diode lets current go in one direction only.

Arduino link: the first starter-kit project ("Blink") is this circuit. An Arduino pin replaces the + rail.

E4 — Series and parallel

Goal: check Kirchhoff's laws.

Parts: battery pack, 330 Ω, 1 kΩ, jumpers.

Predict:

Explain: in series, the two voltages add up to the pack voltage. In parallel, the two branch currents add up to the total current. The larger resistor takes the larger share of the voltage in series. It takes the smaller share of the current in parallel.

Arduino link: several LEDs on one supply are in parallel. Each LED needs its own resistor.

E5 — Voltage divider and potentiometer

Goal: make any voltage between 0 V and 6 V.

Parts: battery pack, two 10 kΩ resistors, 10 kΩ potentiometer, 220 Ω, red LED, jumpers.

Predict: - Two 10 kΩ resistors: V_out = 6 × 10 / (10 + 10) = 3.0 V. - Potentiometer: V_out goes from 0 V to 6 V as you turn the knob. - Potentiometer as a dimmer: the LED current goes from 18.2 mA (knob at one end) to 0.39 mA (other end).

Build (fixed divider): 1. Put one 10 kΩ resistor between the + rail and a10. 2. Put the other 10 kΩ resistor between b10 and the − rail. 3. Turn on the pack. Measure between row 10 and the − rail.

Build (potentiometer): 1. Put the potentiometer legs in e20, e21 and e22. The middle leg (wiper) is in row 21. 2. Put a jumper from a20 to the + rail. Put a jumper from a22 to the − rail. 3. Measure between row 21 and the − rail. Turn the knob slowly from one end to the other.

Build (dimmer): 1. Remove the jumper from a22. The potentiometer is now a variable resistor between row 20 and row 21. 2. Put the 220 Ω resistor between a21 and a25. 3. Put the LED long leg in c25 and the short leg in c26. Put a jumper from a26 to the − rail. 4. Turn the knob. Measure the LED current at the a26 jumper at both ends of the knob travel.

Predicted Measured
Fixed divider 3.0 V
Pot, knob at one end 0 V
Pot, knob at middle 3.0 V
Pot, knob at other end 6.0 V
Dimmer, maximum 18.2 mA
Dimmer, minimum 0.39 mA

Explain: the potentiometer's wiper divides one 10 kΩ track into two parts. Their ratio sets V_out. In the dimmer, the 220 Ω resistor protects the LED when the knob is at 0 Ω.

Arduino link: connect the wiper to pin A0, and analogRead(A0) gives 0–1023. This is the "potentiometer" project in every starter kit.

E6 — Button and pull-down resistor

Goal: control an LED with a button, and see why a digital input needs a pull-down resistor.

Parts: battery pack, push button, 330 Ω, 10 kΩ, red LED, jumpers.

Predict: with the pull-down, the voltage at the input row is 0 V when you release the button and 6 V when you push it.

Check the button first: 1. Put the button across the centre gap, with legs in rows 30 and 32 (columns e and f). 2. Set the meter to continuity. Touch the probes to row 30 and row 32. 3. It must beep only while you push. If it always beeps, turn the button 90° and try again.

Build (button and LED): 1. Put a jumper from a30 to the + rail. 2. Put the 330 Ω resistor between a32 and a36. 3. Put the LED long leg in c36 and the short leg in c37. Put a jumper from a37 to the − rail. 4. Turn on the pack. Push the button.

Build (pull-down): 1. Turn off the pack. Remove the resistor, the LED and the a37 jumper. 2. Put the 10 kΩ resistor between b32 and the − rail. Row 32 is now the "input". 3. Turn on the pack. Measure between row 32 and the − rail. Push and release the button. 4. Remove the 10 kΩ resistor. Measure again.

Released Pushed
With 10 kΩ pull-down
Without pull-down

Explain: with the pull-down, the input always has a defined voltage. Without the pull-down, your meter probably still shows 0 V when you release the button. The reason is that the meter has its own input resistance of about 10 MΩ, and it acts as a pull-down. An Arduino input has a much higher resistance. Without a pull-down, it floats and reads random values.

Arduino link: the "button" project uses exactly this circuit. Most programs use INPUT_PULLUP instead. Then the button connects to GND, and a pushed button reads LOW.

E7 — Capacitor charging

Goal: measure the time constant τ = R × C.

Parts: battery pack, 1000 µF capacitor, 10 kΩ, 1 kΩ, red LED, jumpers, a stopwatch (phone).

Predict: τ = 10 000 Ω × 0.001 F = 10 s.

Time 5 s 10 s 20 s 30 s 50 s
Predicted 2.36 V 3.79 V 5.19 V 5.70 V 5.96 V
Measured

Build: 1. Put the capacitor + leg (long) in c40 and the − leg (short, stripe side) in c41. 2. Put a jumper from a41 to the − rail. 3. Discharge the capacitor: put the 1 kΩ resistor between b40 and b41 for 10 seconds. Then remove it. 4. Put one leg of the 10 kΩ resistor in a40. Do not connect the other leg yet. 5. Connect the meter (DC volts) between row 40 and the − rail. 6. Turn on the pack.

Measure: 1. Put the free leg of the 10 kΩ resistor in the + rail. Start the stopwatch at the same time. 2. Read the meter at 5, 10, 20, 30 and 50 seconds. A video of the meter and the stopwatch makes this easier.

Optional: watch the charge leave. 1. Remove the 10 kΩ resistor. The capacitor keeps its charge. 2. Put the 1 kΩ resistor between b40 and b45. Put the LED long leg in c45 and the short leg in c46. Put a jumper from a46 to the − rail. 3. The LED flashes and fades in about 1–2 seconds. The capacitor supplies the current.

Explain: at first, the current is (6 V) / 10 kΩ = 0.6 mA. As the capacitor voltage increases, the voltage across the resistor decreases, and so does the current. That is why the curve becomes flatter.

Arduino link: a 100 nF capacitor across a button removes contact bounce. Capacitors near a motor keep the supply stable.

E8 — Transistor switch

Goal: control a larger current with a small current.

Parts: battery pack, NPN transistor, 10 kΩ, 330 Ω, red LED, jumpers.

Predict: - Base current: (6 − 0.7) / 10 000 = 0.53 mA. - LED (collector) current: (6 − 2.0 − 0.2) / 330 = 11.5 mA.

Find the pins: read the pin diagram in your transistor's datasheet. For a BC547, with the flat side toward you and the legs down, the order is C, B, E from left to right. For a plastic 2N2222A (PN2222A), the order in the same view is E, B, C.

Build: 1. Put the transistor so that C is in row 50, B is in row 51 and E is in row 52 (column e). 2. Put a jumper from a52 to the − rail. 3. Put the 330 Ω resistor between the + rail and a44. 4. Put the LED long leg in c44 and the short leg in c45. 5. Put a jumper from d45 to d50. 6. Put the 10 kΩ resistor between a51 and a56. 7. Put a jumper in c56 and leave its other end free. This is the "control wire". It does the job of an Arduino pin. 8. Turn on the pack. Touch the control wire to the + rail: the LED turns on. Touch it to the − rail: the LED turns off.

Measure: 1. Base current: put the meter (mA) between the + rail (red probe) and the free end of the control wire (black probe). 2. LED current: remove the d45–d50 jumper. Put the meter between row 45 (red probe) and row 50 (black probe). Connect the control wire to the + rail. 3. Voltage between C and E (rows 50 and 52), with the LED on and with the LED off.

Predicted Measured
Base current 0.53 mA
LED current 11.5 mA
V C–E, LED on ≈ 0.1–0.2 V
V C–E, LED off several volts

Explain: the LED current is about 20 times the base current. The transistor could give more, so it is fully on. When it is fully on, it acts almost like a closed switch.

Arduino link: to drive a motor, a relay or many LEDs, an Arduino pin sends a small current to a transistor base. A motor also needs a flyback diode.

E9 (optional) — Night light

Goal: combine a voltage divider and a transistor. The LED turns on when it is dark.

Parts: the E8 circuit, LDR, 100 kΩ.

Predict: an LDR has about 1–10 kΩ in normal room light and more than 100 kΩ in the dark. The divider (100 kΩ on top, LDR below) gives the base more than 0.7 V only when the LDR resistance is high.

Build: 1. Start with the E8 circuit. Remove the 10 kΩ resistor and the control wire. 2. Put the 100 kΩ resistor between the + rail and a51. 3. Put one LDR leg in b51 and the other leg in the − rail. 4. Turn on the pack. Cover the LDR with your hand.

Measure:

LDR resistance (power off) V at base (row 51) LED
Room light
Covered

Explain: this circuit turns on gradually, not at one exact light level. LDR values vary a lot. If the LED stays on in room light, use a larger top resistor or more light. If it never turns on, use a smaller top resistor, for example 47 kΩ.

Arduino link: with an Arduino, connect the divider to A0. Your program then decides at which light level to turn on the LED. The program can also use a different level to turn the LED off, so it does not flicker at the threshold.
