1. What moves in a wire?

A metal wire contains many electrons that can move freely. Each electron carries a small charge. When charge moves along a wire, we call the movement current.

Current is the amount of charge that passes one point each second. The unit is the ampere (A). One ampere is about 6 × 10¹⁸ electrons per second. Small circuits use milliamperes (mA): 1 mA = 0.001 A. An LED uses about 10–20 mA.

Two facts surprise most people:

  • The wire is always full of charge. A battery does not supply the electrons. It pushes the electrons that are already in the wire.
  • The electrons move slowly, often less than 1 mm per second. But all of them start to move at almost the same time. That is why a lamp turns on immediately.

By convention, we draw current from the + terminal, through the circuit, to the − terminal. Electrons actually move in the opposite direction. This history does not change any calculation, so this primer uses the convention.

Current needs a closed loop. If the loop is open at any point, the current stops everywhere in the loop.

2. What pushes the charge?

A battery gives energy to each charge that passes through it. Voltage is the energy per unit of charge. The unit is the volt (V). One volt means one joule of energy for each coulomb of charge.

Voltage is always measured between two points. "The voltage at this wire" means "the voltage between this wire and a reference point". We usually call the battery's − terminal ground (GND) and give it the value 0 V.

A 4 × AA pack has a voltage of about 6 V between its terminals. When the battery is new, the value is a little higher (6.2–6.4 V). An Arduino works at 5 V, so 6 V is close to the voltage you will use later.

3. What limits the current?

Every part of a circuit opposes current. Resistance is the amount of this opposition. The unit is the ohm (Ω). A resistor is a part made to have one known resistance.

Ohm's law connects the three quantities:

V = I × R (volts = amperes × ohms)

You can use it in three ways:

You know You calculate Example with 6 V and 330 Ω
V and R I = V / R 6 / 330 = 0.0182 A = 18.2 mA
I and R V = I × R 0.0182 × 330 = 6 V
V and I R = V / I 6 / 0.0182 = 330 Ω

A useful shortcut: volts ÷ kilohms = milliamperes. For example, 6 V ÷ 1 kΩ = 6 mA.

A wire has almost zero resistance. If you connect a wire directly between the battery terminals, Ohm's law gives a very large current. This is a short circuit. The wire and the battery become hot, and the battery can be damaged. Do not do this.

4. Where does the energy go?

A resistor changes electrical energy into heat. Power is the rate of energy change. The unit is the watt (W).

P = V × I and, with Ohm's law, P = I² × R = V² / R

Most small resistors have a limit of ¼ W (250 mW). Above this limit, they become too hot.

Resistor across 6 V Current Power
220 Ω 27.3 mA 164 mW (warm, but safe)
330 Ω 18.2 mA 109 mW
1 kΩ 6.0 mA 36 mW
10 kΩ 0.6 mA 4 mW

The rule: a smaller resistance across the same voltage gives more current and more heat.

5. Series and parallel

You can connect two parts in two basic ways.

Series: the parts are in one line. The current has only one path. - The current is the same through each part. - The voltages add up to the supply voltage. - The resistances add up: R_total = R1 + R2.

Parallel: the parts connect between the same two points. The current has more than one path. - The voltage is the same across each part. - The currents add up to the total current. - The total resistance is less than the smallest resistance: 1/R_total = 1/R1 + 1/R2.

Example with 330 Ω and 1 kΩ on 6 V:

Series Parallel
Total resistance 1330 Ω 248 Ω
Total current 4.51 mA 24.2 mA
330 Ω 4.51 mA, 1.49 V 18.2 mA, 6 V
1 kΩ 4.51 mA, 4.51 V 6.0 mA, 6 V

These two rules have names. They are Kirchhoff's laws: 1. Current law: the current into a junction equals the current out of it. Charge does not collect in a wire. 2. Voltage law: around a closed loop, the voltage drops add up to the supply voltage. Energy that the battery gives, the parts use.

6. Diodes and LEDs

A diode lets current go in one direction only. An LED (light-emitting diode) is a diode that gives light.

An LED has two legs: - The anode (+) is the longer leg. - The cathode (−) is the shorter leg, on the side with the flat edge.

If you connect the LED in the wrong direction, no current flows and the LED stays dark. This does not damage it at 6 V.

An LED does not obey Ohm's law. It keeps an almost constant voltage across it, the forward voltage (Vf). For a red LED, Vf is about 2.0 V. Blue and white LEDs have a Vf of about 3 V.

LED colour Vf (typical) Current with 330 Ω on 6 V
Red 2.0 V 12.1 mA
Yellow 2.1 V 11.8 mA
Green 2.2 V 11.5 mA
Blue 3.0 V 9.1 mA
White 3.1 V 8.8 mA

The breadboard game uses these values. Your LEDs can differ by 0.1–0.2 V. Measure them in E3.

If you connect an LED directly to 6 V, nothing limits the current. The LED fails in a fraction of a second. Always put a resistor in series with an LED. Calculate the resistor like this:

R = (V_supply − Vf) / I_wanted = (6 − 2) / 0.012 ≈ 330 Ω

The resistor gets the remaining 4 V. With 330 Ω, the current is (6 − 2) / 330 = 12.1 mA.

Resistor LED current Brightness
220 Ω 18.2 mA bright (near the 20 mA limit)
330 Ω 12.1 mA normal
1 kΩ 4.0 mA dim, but clearly on

7. Voltage dividers and potentiometers

Two resistors in series divide the supply voltage. The voltage at the point between them is:

V_out = V_supply × R2 / (R1 + R2)

R2 is the resistor between the output and GND. With two 10 kΩ resistors on 6 V, V_out = 3.0 V.

A potentiometer ("pot") is a voltage divider with a knob. It has three legs. The two outer legs connect to the ends of one resistor track. The middle leg (the wiper) touches the track at the knob position. If you connect the outer legs to 6 V and GND, the wiper gives any voltage from 0 V to 6 V.

Arduino link: analogRead() measures a voltage between 0 V and 5 V and gives a number from 0 to 1023. Many sensors are voltage dividers. One resistor in the divider changes with light, temperature or position.

8. Switches and pull-down resistors

A push button closes the circuit while you push it. To light an LED with a button, put the button in series with the resistor and the LED.

An Arduino input is different. It does not use current; it only measures voltage. If a button connects the input to 5 V, what does the input see when you release the button? It sees nothing. The input is not connected to anything. We say it is floating. A floating input can read HIGH or LOW at random, because it picks up electrical noise.

The solution is a pull-down resistor (for example, 10 kΩ) from the input to GND: - Button released: the resistor holds the input at 0 V (LOW). - Button pushed: the button connects the input to 5 V (HIGH). Only 0.5 mA goes through the 10 kΩ resistor.

A pull-up resistor does the opposite: it connects to 5 V, and the button connects to GND. The Arduino has internal pull-up resistors (pinMode(pin, INPUT_PULLUP)). With a pull-up, a pushed button reads LOW.

9. Capacitors

A capacitor stores charge. It has two metal plates with an insulator between them. Current cannot go through the insulator. But charge can collect on one plate and leave the other plate.

When you connect a capacitor to a battery through a resistor: 1. At first, the capacitor is empty. The current is large. 2. As the charge increases, the capacitor voltage increases. The current decreases. 3. At the end, the capacitor voltage equals the battery voltage. The current stops.

The speed of this change depends on the time constant:

τ = R × C (seconds = ohms × farads)

After one τ, the capacitor is at 63 % of the supply voltage. After 5 τ, it is almost full (99 %). With 10 kΩ and 1000 µF, τ = 10 000 × 0.001 = 10 seconds. This is slow enough to measure with a stopwatch:

Time 5 s 10 s 20 s 30 s 50 s
Capacitor voltage 2.36 V 3.79 V 5.19 V 5.70 V 5.96 V

Polarity: the capacitors in kits (electrolytic capacitors) have a + and a − leg. The − leg is shorter and is marked with a stripe. Connect them in the correct direction. Use a capacitor rated for at least 10 V.

Arduino link: capacitors smooth the supply voltage. They also remove "bounce" from buttons and set the timing in many circuits.

10. Transistors as switches

An Arduino pin can supply only about 20 mA safely. A motor or a strip of LEDs needs much more. A transistor lets a small current control a large current.

An NPN transistor has three legs: - Collector (C): the large current goes in here. - Emitter (E): the large current goes out here, to GND. - Base (B): the small control current goes in here.

When a small current flows into the base, the transistor lets a much larger current flow from the collector to the emitter. The ratio is the gain (hFE). It is usually more than 100. When there is no base current, the transistor is off.

Always put a resistor in series with the base. The base–emitter junction is a diode with about 0.7 V across it.

Example with 6 V, a 10 kΩ base resistor, and the LED with 330 Ω on the collector:

  • Base current: (6 − 0.7) / 10 000 = 0.53 mA
  • Collector current: (6 − 2.0 − 0.2) / 330 = 11.5 mA

The base current is 22 times smaller than the LED current. The transistor could give more current, so it is fully on ("saturated"). The 0.2 V is the small voltage that remains across a fully-on transistor.

Check the pins of your transistor. A BC547 and a 2N2222 have their pins in different orders. Find the datasheet and read the pin diagram.

Arduino link: a motor also needs a flyback diode. The workbook does not use a motor, but the starter kit does.

The water analogy, and where it fails

Many people find it useful to compare a circuit with water in pipes. Use the analogy to guess, and use Ohm's law to check.

Electricity Water
Charge Water
Current (A) Flow rate (litres per second)
Voltage (V) Pressure difference between two points
Resistance (Ω) A narrow section of pipe
Battery Pump
Diode One-way (check) valve
Capacitor A tank with a rubber wall across the middle
Transistor A large valve that a small pipe opens

Where the analogy fails: - An open circuit does not leak. If you cut a pipe, water comes out. If you cut a wire, the current stops. - The energy does not travel with the electrons. The electric field carries it, almost at the speed of light. The electrons only drift slowly. - An LED is not a narrow pipe. It keeps an almost constant voltage, which no simple pipe does.
